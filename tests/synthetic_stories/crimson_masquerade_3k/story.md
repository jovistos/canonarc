She had trusted him implicitly. When the northern envoys delayed, when the grain accounts failed to balance, when the pact itself seemed ready to tear apart in their hands, it had been Julian who calmed her with his careful arithmetic and his assurances that every column could be made to reconcile. He never once looked away from her, and she had taken that steadiness as devotion. She remembered the way he had smiled then, and how she had mistaken his nervousness for diligence.

The memory came back now with the smell of cold wax and wet wool. Elena had signed the pact beneath the great chandelier while snow whispered against the windows, and Julian had pressed the seals into place one by one. She had believed the ceremony marked an end to suspicion. Instead it had planted the seed of everything now rotting in the vault below. The winter crisis had taught her to trust, and that trust had been the most expensive lesson of her life.

Standing alone in the Council Chamber, Elena felt the old cold return. The candles seemed to burn lower, the velvet darker, and for a moment she was again the woman who had believed a pact could be kept by parchment alone. Then the rain rattled the balcony doors, and the present pressed back in—furious, patient, and waiting.
