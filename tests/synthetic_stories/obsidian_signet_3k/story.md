### Chunk 1: Assembly & Disguised Courier Briefing
*Scene: `scene_1_assembly` | Chapter: `ch_1` | Location: `loc_council` | Time: `day_1_dusk` | Length: 359 words (~487 tokens)*

The Council Chamber had soured with candle smoke. Forty tallow candles burned along the long oak table, their flames bowing each time the great doors breathed, and the shadows of four people slid across the polished grain like conspirators who had not yet chosen sides. Elena sat at the head of the table, her hands folded over a lacquered case of sealed reports, calm as a frozen lake under winter starlight. Beside her, the old general Marcus sprawled in his chair with the easy arrogance of a man who had never lost a war he cared about, one boot hooked over the table's edge, wine untouched at his elbow. Across from him the young treasurer Julian perched on the forward inch of his seat, fingers laced tight, jaw working silently as though he were chewing a stone. And behind them all, near the door, stood the hooded courier: grey wool, a scarf drawn across the mouth, eyes watchful inside the hood's shadow, saying nothing at all.

Elena lifted the case and set it before Marcus with a soft click of the brass clasp. "Molim vas da pažljivo pregledate ove poverljive izveštaje pre nego što kraljevska straža zatvori kapije." Her voice carried no heat, only the flat weight of a woman accustomed to being obeyed before the sentence ended. Marcus grunted, thumbed the wax seals one by one, and did not look up.

The hooded courier stepped forward then, boots whispering on the flagstones, and bowed low enough that the hood swallowed the candlelight. "Ponizno vam donosim zapečaćene depeše sa severne granice koje zahtevaju vašu hitnu vojnu procenu." The voice was muffled, carefully flattened, neither young nor old. Elena's gaze drifted to the courier's gloves — fine leather, city-stitched, far too costly for a border rider — and something behind her eyes went very still.

Julian cleared his throat twice before any sound came out. "Yes, indeed." The two words fell into the silence and died there, absurd and small. A candle spat. Marcus turned a page. The courier straightened, hands folded, and watched Julian the way a hawk watches a mouse pretending to be a stone.

Marcus read for a long while, and the chamber grew hotter and closer with every page. "The air in here is a dead man's breath," he announced, and no one disagreed. Behind them, in the shadows by the door, the hooded courier had not moved an inch. The wool hood stayed lowered, the grey scarf stayed drawn, and the courier's eyes went from Elena to Julian and back again, quiet and watchful, hoarding every word like a coin nobody would ever get back.

"Empty them," Elena said. "Every pocket. Every fold. Now." She held out one hand, palm up, and her rings caught the candlelight like a row of tiny executions. Julian's breath came in short, ragged pulls. He fumbled at his coat as though his fingers had forgotten their business, and for one long moment the whole chamber seemed to hang suspended between one heartbeat and the next.

Then he moved. Not toward Elena — toward the courier. "Take this cursed ring and hide it deep inside the stone vault before the guards arrive!" His voice cracked on the last word. The courier's fingers closed around the Obsidian Signet automatically, and Julian was already running — boots skidding on the flagstones, shoulder clipping a side table so that a decanter went over and spilled red across the ledgers.

He wrenched open the low iron door behind the tapestry, the one that led down into the Archive Vault, and vanished into the dark mouth of the stairwell. The hooded courier stood frozen for an instant, staring down at the black ring in the grey glove — then bolted after him, hood flying back in the slipstream, wool cloak snapping.

Elena remained alone in the Council Chamber amid the overturned wine and the guttering candles. She stared at the open vault door, at the darkness beyond it, and her fury slowly hardened into something older and colder: the certainty of a woman watching a decade of trust burn down a stairwell she had built with her own hands.

### Chunk 4: Flashback to Ten Years Ago
*Scene: `scene_2_flashback` | Chapter: `ch_1` | Location: `loc_council` | Time: `10_years_ago` | Length: 304 words (~409 tokens)*

Ten years ago, the same chamber had smelled of pine resin and wet wool instead of sour candle smoke. It was winter then — the winter of the ruined harvest, when the granaries of the north stood empty and the border lords spoke openly of secession over their ale. Elena remembered standing exactly here, at the head of this table, and she remembered being younger in a way that had nothing to do with the mirror. Naive, and trusting, and foolish enough to believe that a signed pact was a wall.

The room had been full of frost-bitten couriers that night, stamping snow from their boots, blowing on their fingers. The historic pact lay open on the oak boards, its ink still wet, eight seals pressed into the wax in a solemn red arc. And at her right hand, steady and earnest, had stood Julian — barely more than a boy, his collar too large for him, his voice shaking only a little as he read the treasury's guarantees aloud to the assembled lords. She had trusted him implicitly. Utterly. That was the thing she remembered most clearly now: the warmth of that trust, how comfortable it had been to wear, like a good winter coat she never once thought to check for holes.

He had brought her tea afterward, she recalled, and taken the blame for a miscounted ledger no one else would claim, and she had thought: here is a man I could build a kingdom with. The snow had beaten against these very windows. The same long oak table. The same high chairs.

Standing alone in that room in the present, Elena could still feel the ghost of that winter draft on her cheek — and behind it, sharper than any winter, the cold knowledge of how thoroughly she had been wrong.

Down in the Archive Vault, the air was stone-cold and tasted of dust and old iron. Julian had backed himself against a shelf of ledgers, chest heaving, when the courier came down the last steps and stopped in the lantern light. Then the grey gloves rose, hooked the scarf, and dragged the wool hood back — and a woman's face looked out of it, hard-jawed and thoroughly determined, dark hair crushed flat by the hood's weight. "Lyra," Julian whispered, shocked, as though the name itself had struck him. "You were— you were a rider from the north, you said—"

"Predaj mi sve lažne povelje odmah, Julijane, ili ćeš do zore biti u kraljevskoj tamnici!" Lyra's hand was already out, palm up, the Obsidian Signet glinting between her fingers like a black eye. Julian flinched as though she'd raised a blade.

Upstairs, the balcony door groaned open and Marcus came back into the Council Chamber, rubbing warmth into his hands, just as Julian stumbled up the vault stair and into the room — cloak torn, hair wild, breathing like a hunted thing. Marcus's eyes narrowed. "What in the nine hells happened to you?" Julian straightened his collar with trembling fingers and delivered his tale: northern raiders had broken into the annex, he said, desperate men with axes, and he had defended the ledger with his life, but the Obsidian Signet was gone.

Elena did not move from the window. Inside, the fury sat in her chest like a hot coal, but her voice came out dry as paper. "Oh, what a wonderfully convenient tale of heroic vigilance, dear cousin." Marcus frowned at her tone, uncertain where the blade was pointed. Lyra stayed below in the vault, listening to the ceiling, saying nothing.

The vault stairwell glowed, and Lyra came up out of it with her hood thrown back and the Obsidian Signet held high in her bare hand. The candlelight slid around the black stone and found no reflection in it at all. Marcus turned, saw the ring, and his weather-beaten face went through surprise, suspicion, and then a slow, terrible comprehension as Lyra spoke — plainly, without ornament — of the pocket emptied in this room, of the ring pressed into a courier's glove, of the stolen charters and the lie about raiders that had been stitched together before she had even reached the top step.

Marcus's jaw set until the muscle jumped beneath his scar. Indignant heat flooded his cheeks, and he wheeled on the young treasurer with a roar that rattled the decanters. "Kako se usuđuješ da obmanjuješ vojni savet dok držimo dokaze u rukama!"

Julian was cornered against the long oak table, three pairs of eyes pinning him there like knives through a moth. His hands opened and closed. And then — with the strange, jerky calm of a man who has prepared for exactly this — he reached inside his collar and drew out a small glass phial of cloudy amber liquid. He drank it in one swallow before anyone could cross the room. The phial fell and shattered.

Julian blinked. Then blinked again, and the terror drained out of his features, replaced by the bland confusion of a sleeper woken in an unfamiliar house. He looked at the ring, at Marcus's fury, at Elena's cold stare, and there was nothing in his face but polite bewilderment. He did not remember the winter pact. He did not remember the snow at the windows, the tea, the trust, the ten years of it. He did not even remember why he was afraid. Elena watched the man she had built a kingdom beside become a stranger in a single breath, and somewhere far below the Council Chamber, the last candle guttered out.
